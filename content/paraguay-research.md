## 1. Geography

### 1.1 Two regions divided by the Paraguay River [S9]
The **Paraguay River** runs north to south and splits the country in two very different halves.

- **Eastern Region (Región Oriental)**
  - About 40% of the land and ~97% of the population `[K]`.
  - Rolling hills, plateaus, fertile farmland, remnants of Atlantic Forest, and all major cities except the Chaco towns.
  - The **Paraná Plateau** covers roughly the eastern third, from Encarnación north to the Brazilian border.
  - Hill ranges include the Cordillera de los Altos (near Asunción), the Ybytyruzú (Guairá), the Amambay and Mbaracayú ranges (the watershed between the Paraguay and Paraná rivers), and the San Rafael range (southeast).
- **Western Region (Región Occidental), the Chaco**
  - About 60% of the land but under 4% of the population.
  - Part of the Gran Chaco, a vast plain shared with Bolivia and Argentina.
- **Highest point** `[!]`: Britannica gives Cerro San Rafael (850 m). Several Paraguayan sources name Cerro Tres Kandú (~842 m, Ybytyruzú). Avoid naming a single "highest peak", or mention both claims.

### 1.2 Rivers [S9]
Rivers trace about four-fifths of Paraguay's border.

| River | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Paraguay** | Central axis of the country. Historic transport route. Asunción sits on its east bank. Low water in recent drought years has disrupted barge traffic `[K]`. |
| **Paraná** | Forms the southern and eastern borders. Site of two binational dams: **Itaipú** (with Brazil) and **Yacyretá** (with Argentina) `[K]`. Encarnación and Ciudad del Este are on its banks. |
| **Pilcomayo** | Border with Argentina in the southwest. Heavily sediment-laden. Its course shifts and it floods the Low Chaco. Joins the Paraguay near Asunción. |
| **Apa** | Northern border with Brazil `[K]`. |
| **Tributaries** | Tebicuary, Monday, Acaray, Ñacunday, Jejuí, Aquidabán `[K]`. |

### 1.3 The Chaco [S9, S28, S44]
- **Climate and terrain:** Hot, flat, semi-arid in the west and wetter in the east. The Chaco has distinct wet and dry seasons, and parts of it are near semi-arid.
- **Low (Humid) Chaco:** East, near the Paraguay River. Palm savanna, grassland, and seasonal floods.
- **Central Chaco:** Home to the Mennonite colonies (Filadelfia, Loma Plata, Neuland) and several Indigenous communities.
- **High (Dry) Chaco:** Northwest. Thorn scrub and dry forest, with sand dunes (médanos) near Bolivia.
- **Paraguayan Pantanal:** Northeast Chaco, Alto Paraguay department. Part of the world's largest tropical wetland (§5).
- **Importance:** The Dry Chaco is the second-largest forest biome in South America after the Amazon (S28). It has one of the highest deforestation rates in the world (§5.4).
- **History:** Site of the Chaco War (1932–35) (§4.5).

### 1.4 Atlantic Forest (Bosque Atlántico del Alto Paraná) [S25, S26]
- **Extent:** Subtropical humid forest that once covered most of eastern Paraguay.
- **Loss:** Over 90% of the Paraguayan portion has been cleared, mostly since the 1970s, for soy, cattle, and settlement.
- **Largest surviving block:** Mbaracayú Forest Nature Reserve (64,405 ha).
- **Other remnants:** San Rafael (Itapúa/Caazapá), Ybycuí, Ñacunday, and the Itaipú reserves `[K]`.

### 1.5 Wetlands [S23, S24, S31, S32]
- **Pantanal:** Paraguay's share lies in the far north (Río Negro, Bahía Negra area). It is on UNESCO's Tentative List (S32).
- **Ñeembucú wetlands:** Southwest. Esteros, lagoons, and floating vegetation.
- **Lago Ypoá National Park:** Created 1992, ~100,000 ha, Ramsar site since 1995.
- **Ramsar sites:** Paraguay has six, including Lago Ypoá and Tinfunqué (Chaco).
- **Lake Ypacaraí:** Cordillera/Central departments. Culturally famous (song "Recuerdos de Ypacaraí"). Water quality is periodically affected by algal blooms `[K]` `[!]`.

### 1.6 Other notable natural areas (see §5.2 for the full table)
Laguna Blanca (San Pedro): a clear, sandy-bottomed lake in cerrado landscape [S39b]. Cerro Corá (Amambay). Chaco parks (Defensores del Chaco, Médanos del Chaco, Tinfunqué). San Rafael.

## 2. Destinations

Ordered roughly by usefulness to international travellers. Distances and times are approximate `[K]`. Ask users to check current access, hours, and fees.

### Asunción (capital)
- **Founded:** 15 August 1537, on the Paraguay River. One of the oldest cities in South America. Often called the "Mother of Cities" because colonists from Asunción founded other settlements, including the second founding of Buenos Aires in 1580 `[K]`.
- **Key sights:**
  - Panteón Nacional de los Héroes (national mausoleum).
  - Casa de la Independencia: a 1772 house where the May 1811 independence meetings took place, now a museum.
  - Palacio de los López (presidential palace, 19th c.) `[K]`.
  - Museo del Barro: folk art, Indigenous art, and contemporary art. One of the best cultural museums in the country `[K]`.
  - The Costanera riverfront.
  - Loma San Jerónimo, a colourful hillside neighbourhood `[K]`.
  - Mercado 4, the large popular market; also sells pohã ñana herbs `[K]`.
- **Metro area:** The Central department around Asunción (Luque, San Lorenzo, Lambaré, etc.) holds most of the country's people. The airport is in Luque. (S7)

### Ciudad del Este (Alto Paraná)
- **Profile:** Second-largest city. Founded 1957 as Puerto Flor de Lis `[K]`. Sits on the Triple Frontier with Foz do Iguaçu (Brazil) and Puerto Iguazú (Argentina).
- **Border crossing:** The Friendship Bridge (Puente de la Amistad) links it to Brazil.
- **Main draws:**
  - Duty-free-style shopping.
  - **Itaipú Dam**: a binational Paraguay–Brazil hydroelectric plant and one of the largest in the world by output. Offers guided tours and a night light show (check current schedule at S63) `[!]`.
  - Nearby **Saltos del Monday** and **Iguazú Falls** (in Argentina/Brazil, an easy trip from here).
- **Safety note:** Alto Paraná is flagged for increased caution in the US advisory (as of 2026) `[!]`.

### Encarnación (Itapúa)
- **Location:** On the Paraná, facing Posadas (Argentina) across the San Roque González de Santa Cruz bridge `[K]`.
- **Waterfront:** The reservoir of the Yacyretá dam raised the river level. The city rebuilt its waterfront as a long **Costanera** with public beaches, the most popular being **Playa San José**. It markets itself as the "summer capital".
- **Carnival:** Paraguay's biggest (summer, usually January–February; dates vary yearly `[!]`).
- **Gateway to the missions:** Base for the Jesuit missions of **Trinidad** and **Jesús** (UNESCO) and **San Cosme y Damián**, all within about an hour `[K]`.

### Jesuit Missions: Trinidad and Jesús de Tavarangue (Itapúa) [S1]
- **UNESCO status:** World Heritage Site since 1993.
- **Background:** Part of a network of about 30 Guaraní *reducciones* run by the Jesuits in the Río de la Plata basin in the 17th–18th centuries. Seven were in present-day Paraguay.
- **Trinidad:** Founded 1706. The best-preserved and most ambitious complex. Its stone church (c. 1745) has a carved frieze of angels playing musical instruments.
- **Jesús de Tavarangue:** Its church was never finished. It is notable for its trefoil (three-lobed) arches, which show Moorish/Mudéjar influence `[K]`.
- **Style:** Both combine Indigenous craftsmanship with Baroque and other European influences.
- **Night visits:** Trinidad has offered night sound-and-light visits `[!]`.

### San Cosme y Damián (Itapúa) [S33, S34]
- **History:** Jesuit mission founded 1632 (relocated later `[K]`). On UNESCO's Tentative List.
- **Astronomy:** Linked to Buenaventura Suárez, a Jesuit astronomer who made observations in the missions for decades in the 18th century.
- **Today:** A modern astronomy/visitor centre operates here `[!]`.

### Areguá (Central) [S61]
- **Location:** Hillside town on Lake Ypacaraí, roughly 30 km from Asunción.
- **Known for:** Pottery and ceramics (many family workshops line the main road), strawberries (season roughly July–October, with a strawberry fair), a hilltop church, and old summer villas.
- **Good for:** An easy day trip for arts and crafts.

### San Bernardino (Cordillera) [S61]
- **History:** Founded 24 August 1881 by German and Swiss immigrants on the east shore of Lake Ypacaraí. Named in honour of President Bernardino Caballero.
- **Today:** A classic summer resort for Asunción residents, with lakeside villas, clubs, and summer nightlife. Much quieter out of season.

### Filadelfia and the Mennonite Chaco (Boquerón) [S35, S36]
- **Founding:** Filadelfia was founded in 1930 as the centre of **Fernheim Colony** ("home far away"). The settlers were Mennonites of Russian-German origin who fled the Soviet Union.
- **Neighbouring colonies:** Menno (Loma Plata, settled 1927 by Mennonites from Canada `[K]`) and Neuland (1947 `[K]`).
- **Profile:** Largest town in the central Chaco and a base for Chaco trips. It has museums on colony history and Chaco nature, and an ethnographic museum on the region's Indigenous peoples.
- **Languages:** Spanish, German, Plautdietsch, Guaraní, and Indigenous languages are spoken.
- **Framing:** Present the Mennonites as one of several Chaco communities alongside the Nivaclé, Enlhet, and others. Avoid "they civilised the empty Chaco" narratives: the land was already inhabited, and relations and land use are debated.

### Ybycuí National Park (Paraguarí) [S41]
- **Park:** Created 1973. About 5,000 ha of Atlantic Forest in hilly terrain, with streams and waterfalls (Salto Mina, Salto Guaraní, and others).
- **La Rosada ironworks:** Ruins of an iron foundry built in the 1850s under Carlos Antonio López as part of his industrialisation drive (among the earliest in South America). Brazilian forces destroyed it in 1869. A small museum is on site.
- **Good for:** Combining nature and history in one visit.

### Saltos del Monday (Presidente Franco, Alto Paraná) [S40]
- **The falls:** About 45 m high and 120 m wide, near where the Monday River joins the Paraná.
- **Visiting:** 10–15 km from Ciudad del Este `[K]`, with viewing platforms and some adventure activities `[!]`.

### Ñacunday National Park (Alto Paraná) [S22, S40]
- **Park:** Small (~2,000 ha), protecting Atlantic Forest.
- **Salto Ñacunday:** About 40 m high and 110 m wide (heights vary by source).
- **Access:** More remote than Monday. Road conditions vary `[!]`.

### Cerro Akatĩ (Guairá) [S42]
- **Note:** It is in **Guairá**, not Paraguarí, and is often spelled *Akatí / Akati*.
- **Location:** A peak in the **Ybytyruzú** range, near Colonia Independencia and Villarrica. Elevation roughly 700 m `[!]`.
- **The hike:** Popular for its viewpoint over the Guairá countryside. Paths are partly unshaded and not always waymarked, so bring water and an offline map. Hiring a local guide is recommended.
- **Nearby:** Colonia Independencia is a farming town of German descent, known for wine and cider production `[K]`.

## 3. Culture

> **Tone rule:** Describe living practices, not an exotic past. Distinguish the **Guaraní language and national culture** (shared by most Paraguayans) from **Indigenous Guaraní peoples** (distinct communities facing land and poverty problems). Avoid "untouched", "hidden gem", "noble savage", and "frozen in time".

### 3.1 Guaraní and bilingualism [S21, S57]
- **Language family:** Guaraní belongs to the Tupí-Guaraní family.
- **Unusual in the Americas:** An Indigenous language spoken by a *majority* of the non-Indigenous population, not only by Indigenous communities.
- **Legal status:** The **1992 Constitution** declares Paraguay pluricultural and bilingual, with Spanish and Guaraní as co-official. A 2010 Language Law created a language-policy secretariat and a Guaraní Language Academy `[K]`. Bilingual schooling is required.
- **How many speak it:** Figures vary by census and question wording. Roughly 70–80% understand or speak Guaraní, and roughly half the population is bilingual. Most people fall on a spectrum rather than in "Guaraní-only" or "Spanish-only". Give ranges, not one precise figure `[!]`.
- **Jopara:** Everyday speech often mixes Spanish and Guaraní.
- **Status history:** Spanish was historically the language of administration and prestige, and Guaraní was the home and rural language. Guaraní faced stigma for a long time. Its status has risen, but the social gap persists.
- **Distinctions:** Paraguayan Guaraní differs from the languages of Indigenous Guaraní peoples (Mbya, Avá, Paĩ Tavyterã, etc.).
- **Useful phrases** `[K]`:
  - *Mba'éichapa* — hello / how are you
  - *Aguyje* — thank you
  - *Jajotopata* — see you later

### 3.2 Paraguayan identity (factual framing)
- **Roots:** Largely mestizo. Colonial society formed through Spanish–Guaraní unions (S60).
- **Key threads of identity:**
  - The Guaraní language
  - Memory of the two great wars (Triple Alliance and Chaco)
  - Popular Catholicism (Caacupé, patron-saint festivals)
  - Rural and family life
  - Shared customs such as tereré
- **Immigrant communities** `[K]`:
  - Italian, German and Swiss, Spanish
  - Mennonite (Chaco)
  - Japanese (La Colmena, founded 1936)
  - Korean, Lebanese and Syrian
  - Ukrainian and other Slavic groups (Itapúa)
  - Brazilian ("brasiguayos") in the east
- **Afro-Paraguayans:** A small but historic community, including Kamba Cuá near Asunción and Emboscada `[K]`.
- **Contested memory:** The meaning of the Triple Alliance War, Francia, the López family, and Stroessner is politically contested (§4). Present these as debated, not settled.

### 3.3 Indigenous heritage [S5, S6, S62]
- **Numbers (2022 Indigenous census):** 140,206 people, 2.29% of the population, belonging to **19 peoples** in **5 linguistic families**.

| Family | Share | Peoples |
|---|---|---|
| Guaraní | 55.6% | Mbya, Avá Guaraní, Paĩ Tavyterã, Aché, Guaraní Ñandeva, Western Guaraní |
| Lengua Maskoy | 23.1% | Enlhet Norte, Enxet Sur, Sanapaná, Angaité, Toba Maskoy, Guaná |
| Mataco Mataguayo | 15.2% | Nivaclé, Maká, Manjui |
| Zamuco | 3.6% | Ayoreo, Ybytoso, Tomárãho |
| Guaicurú | 1.6% | Qom |

- **Largest peoples:** Mbya Guaraní (20.4%), Avá Guaraní (16.5%), Nivaclé (13.3%), Paĩ Tavyterã (11.4%).
- **Isolated groups:** Groups of **Ayoreo Totobiegosode** in the northern Chaco live in voluntary isolation. They are the only such people in South America outside the Amazon basin. Deforestation threatens their territory (S62).
- **Current issues** (S6): land titling and restitution, deforestation, poverty, and access to health and education.
- **Site guidance:** Present Indigenous peoples as contemporary communities. Use their own names, not generic "the Indians". Link to community-run crafts and tourism where they exist.

### 3.4 Music [S3, S4, S56]
- **Paraguayan harp (arpa paraguaya):** Widely regarded as the national instrument. Descended from European harps introduced in the colonial era. The Jesuit missions played a role in spreading it, though the exact lineage is debated `[!]`.
- **Guitar:** Central to popular and classical traditions. **Agustín Pío Barrios "Mangoré"** (1885–1944) is Paraguay's most celebrated classical guitarist-composer `[K]`.
- **Polca paraguaya:** Developed in the 19th century. Despite the name, it is rhythmically distinct from the European polka, mixing 6/8 and 3/4 time `[K]`. Variants include galopa and kyre'ỹ.
- **Guarania:**
  - A slower, melancholic genre created by **José Asunción Flores** in 1925, first presented with "Jejuí".
  - **Inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage on 3 December 2024**, at the committee session held in Asunción.
  - Best-known songs: "India" and "Recuerdos de Ypacaraí" `[K]`.

### 3.5 Dance [K]
- **Danza de la botella:** Dancers balance one or more bottles on their heads. The best-known folk dance.
- **Galopa:** Danced by *galoperas* at patron-saint festivities, often with jars (cántaros) on their heads.
- **Performance context:** Folk ballet groups perform stylised versions. Traditional dress is the *typói* blouse with ñandutí or ao po'i details.

### 3.8 Everyday customs [S2, S20, K]
- **Tereré and mate:** One shared *guampa* (cup) and *bombilla* (metal straw) go around the group. The server refills and passes it. Say "gracias" only when you want to stop being served.
- **Sunday asado:** A family and social ritual.
- **Siesta:** Quiet midday hours are common outside the big cities, especially in heat.
- **Greetings:** A single cheek kiss is common in informal settings. Handshakes are used in business.
- **Religion:** Mostly Catholic, with a growing Protestant/evangelical presence `[K]`.

## 4. History

Each era below separates **Facts** (well documented) from **Interpretations** (debated). Present debates as debates.

### 4.1 Before 1500 (pre-colonial)
**Facts**
- Humans have lived in the region for thousands of years `[K]`.
- **Eastern region:** Guaraní-speaking peoples practised slash-and-burn farming (maize, cassava, beans, squash) and lived in village communities `[K]`.
- **Chaco:** Peoples of other language families (the ancestors of today's Nivaclé, Enlhet, Ayoreo, Qom, and others) lived as hunter-gatherers and seasonal farmers `[K]`.
- **Population:** Estimates for the area of modern Paraguay at contact are uncertain. Figures around 100,000–200,000 appear in the literature `[!]`.
- **Food legacy:** Many staples of today's cuisine, including cassava, maize, and yerba mate use, have Guaraní origins (S54).

**Interpretations**
- Claims of a single unified "Guaraní nation" oversimplify. Guaraní groups were politically decentralised `[K]`.

### 4.2 Colonial period (1537–1811)
**Facts**
- **1537:** Asunción founded (15 August). It became the hub of Spanish expansion in the region.
- **Spanish–Guaraní alliances:** Early alliances included unions between Spanish men and Guaraní women. They produced the mestizo, Guaraní-speaking society that shaped Paraguay (S60).
- **Forced labour:** Encomienda labour systems exploited Guaraní people `[K]`.
- **1609–1767:** The Jesuits founded *reducciones* (mission towns) that gathered tens of thousands of Guaraní, until the Jesuits were expelled in 1767 (S1, S60).
- **1717–1735:** The Comuneros revolt in Asunción challenged colonial authorities `[K]`.

**Interpretations**
- **The Jesuit missions** are portrayed both as a "utopia" that protected Guaraní from enslavement and as instruments of cultural and religious transformation. Most historians see both elements. Avoid the word "utopia" as fact.

### 4.3 Independence and the early republic (1811–1862)
**Facts**
- **Independence:** 14–15 May 1811. Paraguay rejected both Spanish rule and Buenos Aires' authority. Earlier that year it had defeated an expedition from Buenos Aires led by Belgrano `[K]`.
- **José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia ("El Supremo"):** Ruled 1814–1840 (dictator from 1814, for life from 1816 `[K]`). He isolated the country, restricted trade and travel, and suppressed the elite and the Church.
- **Carlos Antonio López (1844–1862):**
  - Opened the country and pursued state-led modernisation.
  - Projects included the railway (one of South America's earliest, 1861 `[K]`), the telegraph, shipyards, and the La Rosada ironworks at Ybycuí (S41).

**Interpretations**
- Francia is seen as a nationalist defender of sovereignty, or as a tyrant, or both.
- The claim that pre-war Paraguay was an "egalitarian, self-sufficient model state" comes from later revisionist and nationalist writing and is disputed.

### 4.4 War of the Triple Alliance (1864–1870) [S10, S11, S59]
**Facts**
- **Sides:** Paraguay, under **Francisco Solano López**, fought Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay.
- **Start:** The war grew out of a crisis in Uruguay and regional rivalry. Paraguay's attacks on Brazilian and then Argentine territory began the fighting.
- **Key sites:** Humaitá fortress, Curupayty (a Paraguayan victory, 1866), and the Piribebuy and Acosta Ñu battles (1869) `[K]`.
- **End:** The war ended when López was killed at **Cerro Corá on 1 March 1870**.
- **Aftermath:** Paraguay lost territory to Argentina and Brazil and was occupied for several years (until about 1876 `[K]`).
- **Losses:** Losses were catastrophic, but the exact figure is **debated**:
  - Many historians estimate Paraguay lost roughly **half or more** of its pre-war population (Whigham & Potthast: ~60–69%).
  - A revisionist demographic study (Reber, *HAHR* 1988) argued for **~8–19%**.
  - Sources disagree on the pre-war population baseline.
  - For the site, use "a large share of its population, with estimates ranging widely and often exceeding half" rather than one number.

**Interpretations**
- **Blame:** Opinions differ on who is to blame. López is seen as a reckless aggressor, as a national hero, or both. Brazil and Argentina are seen as having pursued expansionist aims.
- **"British imperialism caused the war":** Popular in 1960s–70s revisionist writing. Most modern historians consider it unsupported.
- **"Only women and children survived":** An exaggeration. Say instead that adult men were disproportionately killed, and that women played a central role in rebuilding.

### 4.5 Chaco War (1932–1935) [S12]
**Facts**
- **Parties and cause:** Bolivia and Paraguay fought over the Chaco Boreal.
- **Losses:** Around 100,000 people died in total (both sides).
- **Course:** Paraguay, though smaller, adapted better to the terrain and supply conditions and won most of the fighting.
- **End:** Armistice **12 June 1935**. A peace treaty was signed in Buenos Aires on **21 July 1938**.
- **Outcome:** Paraguay kept most of the disputed territory. Bolivia gained a corridor to the Paraguay River.
- **Key figures and places:** Battle of Boquerón (1932) `[K]`. Commander José Félix Estigarribia `[K]`.

**Interpretations**
- **"The war was caused by oil companies":** Frequently cited but debated. Oil was a factor in perceptions, but border disputes long predate it.
- **Mennonite and Indigenous roles:** Both Mennonite and Indigenous communities of the Chaco were affected by the war and played roles in it. This is often under-told.

### 4.6 20th century (1936–1989)
**Facts**
- **1936–1954:** Political instability, including the February Revolution (1936) and a civil war in 1947 `[K]`.
- **1954:** General **Alfredo Stroessner** seized power. He ruled until **3 February 1989** (over 34 years), backed by the army and the Colorado Party (S13).
- **Repression:** Political persecution, torture, disappearances, and exile. Paraguay took part in **Operation Condor**.
- **Economic change:** Large-scale settlement of the eastern frontier and construction of **Itaipú Dam** (treaty 1973; generation began 1984 `[K]`).
- **Archives of Terror:** Police files found in Asunción in December 1992 (Martín Almada was central to their discovery) `[!]` confirm the year. The archives are in UNESCO's Memory of the World register (S14).
  - Some sources give 1991 for the court-ordered seizure. Use "early 1990s", or verify.
- **End of the regime:** Stroessner was overthrown in a coup led by General Andrés Rodríguez in 1989 `[K]`.

**Interpretations**
- Some Paraguayans remember the era nostalgically for its stability and infrastructure. Human-rights findings (S15) document systematic abuse. Present the documented facts and note that public memory remains divided.

### 4.7 Contemporary Paraguay (1989–present)
**Facts**
- **1992:** A new democratic Constitution (bilingualism, rights, no presidential re-election) (S21) `[K]`.
- **2004–2008:** The **Truth and Justice Commission** documented about 10,000 victims of the dictatorship (S15).
- **2008:** Fernando Lugo's election ended about six decades of Colorado Party rule `[K]`.
- **2012:** Congress removed Lugo by impeachment, over roughly two days (S16). Critics and several neighbouring governments called it a "parliamentary coup". Supporters say it followed constitutional procedure. **Present both views.**
- **2023:** Santiago Peña (Colorado) won the April election and took office on 15 August 2023 (S17, S64).
- **Economy** (S18, S19):
  - A major exporter of **soybeans, beef, and hydroelectricity** (Itaipú, Yacyretá).
  - Comparatively fast growth over recent decades.
  - Challenges: inequality, informality, deforestation, and concerns about organised crime in some border regions.
- **Foreign policy:** One of the few countries that recognise Taiwan (as of 2026 `[!]`).

### 5.3 Wildlife highlights [S27, S31, S43, K]
- **Mammals:**
  - **Jaguar**
  - **Chacoan peccary (tagua)**: known only from fossils until living animals were documented in the 1970s. An endemic flagship of the Chaco.
  - Giant anteater, giant armadillo, maned wolf, lowland tapir, marsh deer, capybara, puma, guanaco (Chaco dunes)
- **Reptiles:** Yacaré caiman
- **Birds:**
  - About **690–715 species** recorded; counts vary by source.
  - Highlights: hyacinth macaw (Pantanal), greater rhea (*ñandú*), jabiru stork, toucans.
  - **Bare-throated bellbird** (*pájaro campana*): often cited as the national bird `[K]`.
  - Paraguay is a growing birding destination.

### 5.4 Conservation considerations
- **Chaco deforestation:** Among the fastest in the world, driven mainly by cattle ranching and some soy. Landsat studies show a large share of forest cleared since the 1980s (S29, S28). Reserve expansions have not stopped the loss (S30, May 2026).
- **Atlantic Forest:** Over 90% lost. A "zero deforestation" law for the Eastern region has been in force since 2004 and renewed several times. It slowed clearing, but illegal logging and cannabis plots remain problems (S25, S26) `[K]` `[!]`.
- **Indigenous land rights:** Closely linked to conservation, especially for the isolated Ayoreo (S62, S6).
- **Water and climate:** Recent droughts and low Paraguay River levels, frequent fires, and pollution and algal blooms in Lake Ypacaraí `[K]`.
- **Protection gaps:** Some parks exist on paper with limited staff or infrastructure. Check access and facilities before recommending visits.

### 5.5 Responsible-tourism guidance (for site copy)
- Visit Chaco and Pantanal parks with **licensed local guides** and a suitable vehicle. Carry ample water and tell someone your route.
- Stay on trails. Don't feed wildlife. Never buy wildlife products (skins, feathers, live animals).
- **Fire:** Don't make open fires in dry season. Wildfires are a serious threat.
- **Indigenous communities:** Visit only by invitation or through community-run initiatives. Ask before taking photos. Buy crafts directly from makers or cooperatives.
- **Local economy:** Support locally owned lodging, including rural *posadas* `[K]`, and local guides.
- **Water quality:** Check current conditions before swimming in lakes, especially Ypacaraí.

## 6. Food and drink

> Staples: **mandi'o** (cassava), maize, **queso Paraguay** (fresh cheese), beef, and yerba mate. Many dishes combine Guaraní ingredients with Spanish additions (cheese, eggs, pork fat, beef) (S54).

| Dish | What it is | Cultural context | Src |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sopa paraguaya** | A savoury, dense cornbread made from cornmeal, onion, cheese, eggs, milk, and fat. The name means "Paraguayan soup", but it is solid. | Standard side at asados and celebrations. Popular stories say a cook made a "soup" too thick. These stories are **legend, not documented history** `[!]`. | S54 |
| **Chipa** | Baked bread of **cassava starch**, cheese, egg, fat, and often anise. Ring or roll shaped. | Everywhere. Sold by *chiperas* on roads and buses. Baked in clay ovens during Holy Week. Many regional variants; the town of Eusebio Ayala is famous for it `[K]`. | S54 |
| **Mbejú** | Flat, griddle-cooked cake of cassava starch, cheese, and fat. Crisp outside, chewy inside. | Breakfast or merienda, with cocido. Considered one of the oldest recipes, with Guaraní roots. | S54 |
| **Vori vori** | Thick soup (usually chicken) with small balls ("bori") of cornmeal and cheese. | Comfort food, especially in winter. | S54 |
| **Pastel mandi'o** | Half-moon pastry of cassava and corn dough filled with seasoned meat, fried. | Street food and festival food, especially San Juan and Holy Week `[K]`. | [K] |
| **Chipa guasu** | Savoury bake of **fresh corn kernels** (not cornmeal), cheese, eggs, and milk. Moister than sopa. "Guasu" means "big". | Asado and holiday side. | S54 |
| **Asado** | Grilled beef (and pork, sausages) over wood or charcoal. | Weekend social ritual, served with boiled mandi'o, sopa, chipa guasu, and salad. Paraguay is a major beef exporter (S18). | [K] |
| **Tereré** | Cold infusion of yerba mate, often with *pohã ñana* (crushed medicinal herbs) in the water. Drunk from a *guampa* with a *bombilla*. | **UNESCO Intangible Heritage (2020)**. Shared in a circle with its own etiquette. Herbs are chosen for their traditional healing properties. A strong everyday and national symbol. | S2 |
| **Cocido** | Hot drink of yerba mate toasted with sugar (traditionally caramelised with a hot coal: *cocido quemado*), then boiled. Often with milk. | Breakfast and afternoon, with chipa or mbejú. | [K] |

- **Also worth mentioning** `[K]`: *so'o yosopy* (beef soup), *lampreado*, *kosereva* (bitter-orange sweet), *dulce de mamón*, *mbaipy*.
- **Spelling variants:** mbejú/mbeju, vori vori/bori bori, chipa guasu/guazú, mandi'o/mandió.

## 7. Travel information

> **Everything in this section is time-sensitive.** Show an "information current as of [date], check official sources" note on the site.

### 7.1 Climate and best times [S9, S49, S50]
- **Overall:** Subtropical in the east, tropical and hotter in the Chaco.
- **Summer (Dec–Feb):** Hot and humid. Asunción averages about 28 °C in January, and highs of 38 °C+ are common. The Chaco can exceed 40 °C.
- **Winter (Jun–Aug):** Mild and drier. Asunción averages about 18 °C in July. Occasional cold snaps bring frost to the south and east.
- **Rain:** About 1,300 mm a year in Asunción, heavier in the east and drier in the west Chaco. Peaks come in spring and autumn, with heavy thunderstorms.
- **Best times:**
  - **May–September:** Mild and drier. Best for the Chaco, wildlife, and hiking.
  - **April–May and September–November:** Good all-round shoulder seasons.
  - **December–February:** Hot, but carnival season and lake/beach season.
  - Expect crowds and travel disruption around Semana Santa and 8 December (Caacupé).

### 7.2 Money
- **Currency:** Paraguayan guaraní (PYG, ₲). Notes use large denominations.
- **Payment:** Cash is widely used. Cards are accepted in cities, but less so in rural areas. ATMs are common in towns. US dollars, Brazilian reais, and Argentine pesos can be exchanged at *casas de cambio* in cities and border towns `[K]`.
- **Don't publish exchange rates.**

### 7.3 Languages
- Spanish and Guaraní are both official (§3.1).
- English is limited outside hotels and tourism businesses.
- Portuguese is common near the Brazilian border.
- German and Plautdietsch are spoken in Mennonite areas.

### 7.4 Getting around `[K]` (verify current details)
- **Air:** The main international gateway is **Silvio Pettirossi International Airport (ASU)** in Luque, near Asunción. There is a smaller international airport near Ciudad del Este `[!]`.
- **Bus:** Long-distance buses are the main way to travel between cities and leave from the Asunción bus terminal (Terminal de Ómnibus). There are many companies and comfort classes.
- **Rail:** No regular passenger rail network.
- **In cities:** Ride-hailing apps operate in major cities `[!]`. City buses are cheap but can be confusing for visitors.
- **Driving:** Driving is on the right. Main routes are paved. Chaco parks need 4×4 vehicles, fuel planning, and often a guide. The **Ruta Transchaco (PY09)** is the main road into the Chaco.
- **River:** Boats on the upper Paraguay River serve northern towns and the Pantanal. Schedules are irregular `[!]`.
- **Land borders:** Encarnación–Posadas, Ciudad del Este–Foz do Iguaçu, Pedro Juan Caballero–Ponta Porã, and Asunción–Clorinda.

### 7.5 General considerations
- **Safety:** As of 2026, the US advisory rates Paraguay **Level 1 (normal precautions)**, with **increased caution** for Alto Paraná, Amambay, Canindeyú, Concepción, and San Pedro departments due to crime `[!]` (S46b, not directly accessed; seen via search summary). Petty theft happens in cities. Border areas carry some risk from organised crime.
- **Health** (S45):
  - Mosquito-borne diseases (dengue, chikungunya) cause periodic outbreaks, so use repellent.
  - Protect against heat and sun, and stay hydrated.
  - Routine vaccines, plus hepatitis A and typhoid, are commonly recommended. Yellow fever vaccine may be recommended for some areas.
  - Advise readers to consult a travel clinic.
- **Electricity:** 220 V `[!]` (confirm plug types).
- **Time:** UTC−3 all year (S48).
- **Etiquette:** Accept tereré if offered, but it's fine to decline politely. Dress modestly in churches.

### 7.6 Entry requirements — NOT VERIFIED
- **Status:** Couldn't be checked against official sources. The US State Department and Paraguay's migration office websites were blocked in the research environment, and search summaries contradicted each other on visa rules for some nationalities.
- **What to publish:** Don't state visa rules, fees, or lengths of stay on the site unless you verify them first. Publish a generic statement such as "Entry requirements vary by nationality. Check with Paraguay's Dirección General de Migraciones or your nearest Paraguayan consulate before travel". Link to S47, S46, and S45.
- **Stable background (still verify):** Citizens of Mercosur and associated states can generally enter with a national ID card. Many other nationalities need a passport valid for the stay. Proof of yellow fever vaccination can be required for travellers arriving from at-risk countries `[!]`.
